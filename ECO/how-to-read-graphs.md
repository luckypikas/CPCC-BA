Defining the Terms Correctly
First, let's lock in the terms you asked about:
Axis (Yes, this is the right term!): The lines that form the border of the graph are the axes.
The Horizontal Axis (the flat one going left-to-right) is called the X-axis.
The Vertical Axis (the straight upright one going bottom-to-top) is called the Y-axis. [1, 2]


Line / Curve: The actual line drawn inside the graph that shows the data (like the downward-sloping demand line) is called a curve in economics, even if it is drawn perfectly straight. [1]
Why You Heard It Read Both Ways
In standard algebra and data graphing, the horizontal (X) variable is always mentioned first, followed by the vertical (Y) variable (written as (x, y)). You always mention the cause (independent variable) before the effect (dependent variable). [1]
However, economics graphs have a famous historical quirk: [1]
The "Economics Tradition" Order (Vertical First): In economics, Price (P) is on the vertical axis and Quantity (Q) is on the horizontal axis. Because economists talk about a market by stating a price first and then seeing how much people buy, text and spoken tutorials will very frequently say "Price and Quantity" or read the vertical axis first. [1, 2, 3]
The "Math Rule" Order (Horizontal First): If the video tutorial was focusing on plotting coordinates mathematically, or if the speaker was treating quantity as the starting point, they will switch and say "Quantity and Price" to match the algebraic (x, y) rule. [1]
Because of this conflict between mathematical rules and economic tradition, professors and video creators constantly flip back and forth between saying "Price and Quantity" and "Quantity and Price" without even realizing they are doing it! [1]
Visualizing the Layout
Whenever you look at a supply and demand graph, remember this alphabetical trick to keep yourself grounded: P comes before Q.
P (Price) goes up high on the vertical axis.
Q (Quantity) sits flat on the horizontal axis. [1]

1. The Next Step: The Direction of the Curve (Slope)
Before doing any math, you need to know what the direction of the line tells you. This is called the slope.
Downward Sloping (The Demand Curve): This line goes from the top-left down to the bottom-right. It means as one thing goes up, the other goes down.
🧠 Memory Trick: Downward = Demand. Think of a Downhill slide. If a store jacks up the Price (high on the vertical axis), the Quantity people want to buy slides all the way down.
Upward Sloping (The Supply Curve): This line goes from the bottom-left up to the top-right. It means both variables move in the same direction.
🧠 Memory Trick: Supply = Sky-high. Think of an airplane taking off into the sky. If the Price goes up, businesses want to Supply way more items to make a profit.
2. Shifts vs. Movements (The Ultimate Graph Trap)
This is the single biggest trap students fall into. There is a huge difference between moving along a line and moving the whole line.
Movement ALONG the curve: This happens only when the variable on the vertical axis (Price) changes. You just slide your finger up or down the existing line to a new tick mark.
A SHIFT of the entire curve: This happens when something outside the graph changes (like a sudden trend, a change in weather, or a population jump). The whole line physically jumps to a new spot.
🧠 Memory Trick: Less is Left, More is Right. It doesn't matter if the line is going up or down. If a change makes a quantity increase, push the entire curve to the Right. If it decreases, pull the entire curve to the Left. (Both words share the same starting letters: Less = Left).
3. When to Bring in the Math
Once you can look at a graph and explain the story out loud using these tricks, you are ready for the basic math. You do not need complex calculus. Focus on these three math concepts in this specific order:
Fractions & Percentages (Elasticity): You will use these to see how "sensitive" a curve is. For example, if price goes up by \(10\%\), does the quantity drop a tiny fraction or a massive percentage?
Averages & Totals (Area of a Box): To find things like total revenue or profit on a graph, you often just multiply the height (Price) by the width (Quantity). Visually, you are just finding the area of a rectangle on your grid!
Basic Algebra (Finding the Intercepts): Eventually, lines are written as equations (like P=10-2Q). The math is just a tool to find exactly where the line crosses the tick marks on the vertical and horizontal axes.

When you move past the basic X and Y lines, graphs start using a special visual shorthand—symbols, dotted lines, and letters—to tell a complex story without using any words.
Here are the most important lines, symbols, and labels you will see on graph charts, along with memory tricks to instantly recognize them:
1. The Dotted or Dashed Line (The Guide Rails)
A solid line represents the actual curve or data. A dotted or dashed line is a helper line used to guide your eyes to the answer.
What it does: It typically connects a point inside the graph directly to the tick marks on the vertical and horizontal axes so you can read the exact numbers.
🧠 Memory Trick: Think of dotted lines like laser pointers. If there is an intersection point in space, the dotted lines point straight down to the horizontal axis to show you the Quantity (Q), and straight left to the vertical axis to show you the Price (P).
2. Delta (Δ) — The Triangle Symbol
You will often see a small triangle symbol written next to a letter, like Δ P or Δ Q. This is the Greek letter Delta.
What it means: In math and science charts, Delta means "change in."
How to read it: If a tutorial says Δ P = 5, it simply means "the change in price was 5 dollars."
🧠 Memory Trick: The triangle looks like the point of an arrow. Arrows indicate direction and change!
3. Star or Asterisk (*) — The Sweet Spot
You will frequently see a little star next to a letter or number on the axes, such as P* or Q*, or right next to an intersection point (E*).
What it means: The star stands for Equilibrium or the Optimal Point. It represents the perfect balancing point where the upward line (Supply) and downward line (Demand) cross perfectly.
🧠 Memory Trick: The star means you found the "Star Student" of the graph—the perfect, ideal price and quantity where buyers and sellers are both totally happy.
4. Prime (') — The "After" Picture
Sometimes you will see a little tick mark or apostrophe next to a letter, like D' (read out loud as "D-prime") or P₁'.
What it means: This indicates a new position after a shift has happened. If the original demand curve is labeled D, and a trend causes the whole line to shift to the right, the new line will be labeled D'.
🧠 Memory Trick: Think of the prime mark (') as a dust cloud left behind after a line moves. D is where the line started; D' is where it landed.
5. Subscripts (P₁, P₂) — The Timeline
Instead of prime marks, some charts use little numbers sitting at the bottom right of a letter, like Q₁ and Q₂.
What it means: This shows a sequence of events.
1 always means Before (the starting point).
2 always means After (the next point).
How to read it: If you see an arrow moving from P₁ to P₂, the graph is simply showing you a timeline of how the price moved from its first position to its second position.

PROFILE 1: THE BASICS (FINDING THE PART, TOTAL, OR PERCENT)
This is the core foundation. These codes use the literal "Is", "Of", and "What" triggers.

The Code: "What" (x) "is" (=) \(20\%\) (0.20) "of" () 80.
The Formula: x=0.2080
Hunt Tip: Multiply the decimal straight across.
2. Finding the Percent ("16 is what percent of 80?")
The Code: 16 "is" (=) "what percent" (x) "of" () 80.
The Formula: 16=x80  x=1680
Hunt Tip: Divide the "Is" number by the "Of" number, then move the decimal point two spaces to the right.
3. Finding the Total / Whole ("12 is 30% of what number?")
The Code: 12 "is" (=) \(30\%\) (0.30) "of" () "what" (x).
The Formula: 12=0.30x  x=120.30
Hunt Tip: Divide the part by the percentage decimal to find the large starting number.

PROFILE 2: THE "RETAIL & DISCOUNT" CODES
These puzzles are all about saving or spending money. The trick here is tracking what you PAY vs. what you SAVE.
4. The Simple Discount ("A $50 jacket is 20% off. What is the discount?")
The Code: You are hunting for the value of the cut itself.
The Formula: Discount=0.2050
Hunt Tip: This tells you how much money you don't have to pay.
5. The Sale Price ("A $50 jacket is 20% off. What is the final price?")
The Code: If it is \(20\%\) off, you are keeping and paying \(80\%\) of the original price (100-20=80).
The Formula: SalePrice=0.8050
Hunt Tip: Bypass calculating the discount entirely by multiplying by the percentage you keep.
6. Finding the Original Price ("You bought a pair of shoes on sale for $40, which was 20% off. What was the original price?")
The Code: $40 "is" (=) \(80\%\) (0.80) "of" () the "original price" (x).
The Formula: 40=0.80x  x=400.80
Hunt Tip: Divide the final price paid by the percentage you kept.
7. Wholesale / Retail Markup ("A store buys a rug for $100 and marks it up by 35%. What is the retail price?")
The Code: You are adding to the base \(100\%\). The new price is \(135\%\).

The "Reversibility" Code Crack (The Ultimate Hack): Percentages are completely reversible! \(x\%\) of y is the exact same thing as \(y\%\) of x.

The 10% Anchor Strategy: When hunting for percentages mentally, always find \(10\%\) first by simply sliding the decimal point one spot to the left.

We have covered the most vital conceptual rules for reading and shifting curves at a beginner-to-intermediate level. However, there are two final core rules that tie percentages and graphs together before transitioning into the software:
The Intercept Rule: When a curve hits an axis completely flat, one of your variables is exactly zero. If a demand curve touches the vertical Price axis, it means at that high price, the Quantity demanded is 0.
The Origin Rule: The bottom-left corner where the two axes meet is called the Origin. The value at this point is always (0, 0). The closer a point or line is to the origin, the smaller its numbers are; the further away it stretches, the higher the numbers go.
