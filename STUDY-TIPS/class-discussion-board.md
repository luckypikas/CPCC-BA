Transfer & Career Success - Chapter 2 Section 3: Study Strategies for Student Success


Find your best time and place to study. Some people focus better in the morning, others at night. Study when you have the most energy, and do personal tasks when you don’t.
Break big tasks into smaller steps. Start with the hardest subjects first. Plan and work on big assignments a little at a time instead of all at once, giving your brain a break.
Study in short periods with breaks. Taking breaks helps you not get too tired and gives your brain time to process. Stand up and move around during your breaks.
Choose a good place to study. Study where it’s quiet, well-lit, comfortable, and free of distractions. Put your phone out of sight or on do not disturb.
Write down important dates. Mark test dates, essay deadlines, and personal events on your calendar so you can plan your study time around them. Make changes to that plan as dates change.
Make a study plan. Organize what you need to study, break it into small parts, and set a time limit for each part.
Make time for fun and friends. Don’t just study all the time—have a social life too. Try out leadership activities to build professional skills.
Use the “Twofer” concept, and utilize your waiting time to study. If you’re waiting for something, like laundry or standing in line to buy something, use that time to review your notes or flashcards.
Get enough sleep and eat well. Taking care of your body helps you do everything better -  school, work, and life.

Below is an example of how to get organized and create a study plan based on the course, subject or discipline. For this purpose, we will use History as the course content area. With some minor adjustments, you could create a similar plan for English, psychology or biology.

Prep


Use a special three-ring binder for notes, handouts and assignments
For digital formats, be sure that you’re keeping organized digital files. A folder for each class. Be sure to utilize the college’s M365 cloud management system so nothing gets lost if your device goes down!

Actively engage, listen and answer as many of your questions as possible.
Ask questions and discuss concepts and events with your professors.

Don't forget to find the who, what, where, when, why and how.

Research-Based Study Techniques


The Forgetting Curve


The forgetting curve is a formula by Hermann Ebbinghaus that originated in 1885. Ebbinghaus was a pioneer in the study of memory. His idea was that this curve demonstrated the rate at which information is forgotten over time if we don't attempt to retain it. Ebbinghaus suggested that learners will rapidly lose their memory of learned knowledge in a matter of days or weeks unless the information is reviewed.

This brings us to the learning technique of Spaced Repetition - interrupting the process of forgetting. By spacing out your reviews over several days, retention is higher over time. This is much more effective than cramming the night before a test!

During these Spaced Review sessions, there are several things you can do:

1. Testing: This allows you to retrieve the information over and over so that it is coded more strongly in your memory. This can be done by using flashcards, taking practice tests and quizzes and any other techniques that allow you to test your knowledge.

2. Reflection: This is a powerful tool in which you ask yourself questions like:

How did I do on the last test?
What did I learn?
What went well?
What do I want to do differently?
Reflecting on what you have learned allows you to think about how you might change your strategies for the next time. You can also do this after a class by asking yourself: What did I learn? What concepts were most difficult? Do I still have questions about something?

Test-Taking Strategies


Assessments exist to test your knowledge of a skill or competency in a specific area. Examples of assessments may include quizzes/tests, papers, presentations or projects. Another example might be demonstrating your knowledge or skills in a clinical or lab environment. While you would think that testing and assessment ends in school, many careers require you to continue lifelong learning through continuing education classes, certifications, and on-the-job training. Even your performance review at your job is an assessment of how well you have completed the objectives for your job and performed the necessary skills. Learning the most effective “test-taking” skills now will continue to benefit you throughout your life.

Start Preparing Early


Doing well on assessments starts early in the semester. The instructor may list specifics about tests and assessments in the syllabus along with other key information. As you create a calendar and plan for all the tests and key assessments that will take place in each of your classes, be sure to include the following information:

When are the assessments?
What information will be assessed? Is there a mid-term exam or final? Are they cumulative?
How are these assessments structured? Are they true/false or multiple choice, essay or project-based? Will you be working in a group? Are you allowed to use any notes? Is there a time limit? Will you be taking tests online?
How will they be graded? Is there a rubric that outlines exactly what the instructor expects?
Does the instructor have review sessions?
Once you have a plan, here are some additional tips to help you get ready for tests.

Review your notes and materials without waiting until the last minute. 
Map out the chapter or information or concepts you are reviewing. Mind Mapping can be used to take notes and as an effective review tool. We will learn more about this in our note-taking section.
Teach the material to someone else. While this may happen in a study group, you can also recite the information to yourself in the mirror, to your two-year-old child or to the dog! Teaching someone else is one of the best ways to ensure you know the material.
Try and predict the test questions. Take some time to go through your notes and predict what the instructors may ask. What did they focus on most in class? Did they give a review or study guide? What did they cover on PowerPoint or in their lectures?
Create a game or use online tools such as Quizlet or Generative AI to help you review. Quizlet is an online study tool where you can make flash cards and review games for yourself. You can also use traditional flash cards to help you review and memorize material.
Schedule study time. Remember the time management techniques discussed earlier in this unit. 
Decide in which courses you might benefit from joining a study group or studying with a partner.
Day of the Test or Assessment


Test day has arrived. You have prepared so you feel confident heading into the classroom. Now it is time to practice these additional skills during your assessment.

Use positive self-talk with a good attitude, reminding yourself that you will do your best. It is normal to expect some test anxiety; however, it is imperative to manage your anxiety and keep it to a minimum. Relax, breathe, and channel a positive mental attitude.
Get to class on time. This is not the day to take a new route to school or to try new foods for lunch. Stick to your routine. Eat a well-balanced meal.
Make sure you have the proper materials to take the test. Charge your device.
Read the directions carefully and ask questions if necessary before you get started.
Pace yourself. Don’t worry if someone else finishes first. Take all the time you need.
Build confidence by working on the easier questions first or completing those with the most point value.
If you are having trouble remembering answers, skip those questions and come back later. But be sure to come back and never leave a question unanswered. If you have to guess, tell your teacher what you know.
Avoid making random guesses. Eliminate options you know are incorrect to make a more educated guess.
Try not to second guess or overthink yourself unless you are sure you've misread or misinterpreted the question.
When you finish, use any spare time to go back and re-read your answers, and make sure you have completely answered each question.
Following Up


Sometimes after the test or assessment, consider how to use it as a learning opportunity. Here are some additional tips to help.

Evaluate how you did on the test. Did you feel prepared? Should you do anything differently for the next test in this class? 
If your professor goes over the test, make sure to attend the session. It is helpful to hear what he/she was looking for, as you may be able to use this information on future tests. There also may be times that the instructor will allow points for “corrections,” so be sure to take advantage of any of these opportunities.
Review your test to see the mistakes you made, as learning from them helps you to prepare for future tests. Also review for errors that the instructor may have made in grading.
Read the feedback from the instructor. What specific things did he or she suggest that you can use in future exams? It is also a good idea to meet with the instructor if you have additional questions.
Save your tests to study for midterms and final exams. 


Surprising Tips for Tests


1. Scent Association

Study with a distinct smell and carry the smell with you to the test. Make sure it is not a common smell like perfume that you wear every day or mint.

2. Study Before Bedtime

Get plenty of sleep. Memory is retained during the REM (rapid eye movement) cycles of sleep. The longer you sleep...the more cycles you get.

3. Eat Right

Eat brain foods the day of your test, such as fruits, vegetables and protein. Stay away from carbs and fatty foods that slow your metabolism.

4. Arrive Early

Get to the test location early, especially if this is a different classroom than you normally meet in. It will help you relieve stress and collect yourself.

5. Chew Gum or Mints

Chewing gum sucking on mints right before the test will increase blood flow to the brain.





Mastering Your Memory


A significant part of learning is retaining what you learned. Learning goes beyond simple memorization, but you also must be able to explain, summarize, analyze, and evaluate what you have learned. 

We used one of our memory techniques (creating an acrostic) to learn the following list. Memory is a RARE GIFT and can help you WIN as a student!

Repetition. One good way to remember things is to go over them again and again. The more you see or read something, the easier it is to remember. For example, in 5th grade, you probably memorized multiplication tables by practicing them every week. You can do the same by reading your notes or chapters about World War II more than once. Repeat your vocabulary words out loud. You can also try using another book or website that explains things in a new way. This is also repetition and helps you learn.
Association. Connect new information to what you already know. Our minds like patterns and look for connections. Even old memories can come back if you find something related. Ideas and images in our minds are connected like links in a chain. This is called chunking—grouping information together to make it easier to remember. Chunking works well when you need to learn a lot of new information at once.
Read out loud. There is something about using more than one sense that helps solidify it in your memory. You can read your text out loud, watch a video of the material, or listen to a lecture from class that you have recorded. All these methods are great ways to help you remember.
Enlist your emotions. Make a personal or emotional connection to the material. Finding a way to connect your learning to your personal life, or think about how it makes you feel, can help you recall it later. Reliving the moment creates sensory impressions and are associated in memory and later help remind you of what you have learned.
Grab a game or tell a story. Use Quizlet, create a Jeopardy game or make up crazy stories with the material—the more elaborate the better!
Invent or use mnemonics. A mnemonic is a strategy or tool that aids in the memorization of information. These memory “tricks” can be in the form of acronyms, acrostics, rhymes, or songs.
Find a study partner. Collaborating with others is an effective way to learn material. You can talk it out, quiz each other, compare notes and review for tests together. They may have picked up important information that you did not, or they may have a way to learn those formulas in math that you have not considered!
Teach it to someone. One of the best ways to see if you know material is to teach it to someone else. Explaining an idea to others requires a deeper understanding. This can be a friend, your baby sister, the dog or even yourself in the mirror!
Write it down. Writing things down helps seal them into your memory. Taking notes, rewriting, or editing your notes, or summarizing what you have learned are all useful techniques.
Invent a picture or visualization. Think about what you are learning in psychology and create a picture in your head. Images can be useful when you want to retrieve the information later.
No cramming. Studies show that if you study the material for a bit and then take a break, it allows time for the material to consolidate in your brain. Breaking things into smaller pieces allows you to remember better. The key is to find out what works best for you. Remember memory is a RARE GIFT and can help you WIN as a student!
